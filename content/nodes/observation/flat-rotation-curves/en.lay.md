## What is measured {#observation}

The orbital speed of stars and gas around a galaxy's centre is measured from the Doppler shift of spectral lines: the side turning towards us is shifted to the blue, the receding side to the red. In 1970 Vera Rubin and Kent Ford used this to map regions of ionised hydrogen in the Andromeda galaxy far out from its centre [@rubin-ford-1970 abstract].

If a galaxy contained only the matter we see in stars and gas, orbital speeds in its outer parts should fall, just as distant planets circle the Sun more slowly than nearby ones. For 21 spiral galaxies with radii from 4 to 122 kiloparsecs, Rubin, Ford and Thonnard found instead that the speed stays roughly constant in the outer regions [@rubin-1980 abstract].

The result does not rest on one method or one team. Radio observations of neutral hydrogen reach even further out than optical spectra; a sample of 153 galaxies from the SPARC catalogue shows the same effect, plus a tight relation between the observed acceleration and the acceleration that visible matter alone would produce [@mcgaugh-2016 abstract].

## How it is interpreted {#interpretations}

The observation alone does not say what causes it. The most widespread interpretation assumes each galaxy is surrounded by an extended halo of invisible [[term.dark-matter|dark matter]] — see [[claim.dark-matter-halos]] [@ostriker-1974 abstract; @bertone-hooper-2018 §IV].

A second interpretation, [[theory.mond]], holds that Newton's law does not apply in its usual form at very low accelerations and needs no extra matter; it predicted the shape of the curves for low-surface-brightness galaxies before they were measured [@milgrom-1983 abstract; @mcgaugh-deblok-1998 abstract].

Other observations help decide between the two, such as colliding galaxy clusters or the cosmic microwave background; see the cards [[theory.lcdm]] and [[theory.mond]] [@clowe-2006 abstract; @famaey-mcgaugh-2012 §6].
