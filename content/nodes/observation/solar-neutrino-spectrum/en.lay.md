## What is measured {#observation}

Neutrinos are particles that pass through matter almost unnoticed, so they arrive directly from the Sun's core, where nuclear fusion produces them. John Bahcall calculated the flux that should reach the Earth in 1964 [@bahcall-1964 abstract].

Raymond Davis made the first measurement in the Homestake mine: he caught fewer neutrinos than the calculation expected, and this "solar neutrino problem" lasted more than thirty years [@davis-1968 abstract]. The SNO experiment resolved it in 2002: the total flux of all neutrino types matches the solar model; some electron neutrinos simply turn into other types on the way [@sno-2002 abstract].

The Borexino detector in the Gran Sasso underground laboratory then resolved individual components of the spectrum: neutrinos from the first reaction of the pp chain [@borexino-2014 abstract], all main pp-chain components at once [@borexino-2018 abstract] and, in 2020, neutrinos from the CNO cycle, which supplies about 1% of the Sun's energy [@borexino-2020 abstract].

## How it is interpreted {#interpretations}

The standard solar model reads the measured fluxes as direct evidence that the Sun shines through hydrogen fusion in its core; the "missing" neutrinos turned out to be a gap in particle physics, not in the solar model [@sno-2002 abstract; @borexino-2018 abstract].

The "electric Sun" model from the [[theory.electric-universe]] circle rejects core fusion; its prediction and the outcome are described on the card [[pred.eu-sun-no-fusion-neutrinos]] [@scott-2006 chapter on the Sun; @borexino-2020 abstract].
