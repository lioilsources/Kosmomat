## What it is {#what}

Every particle has an antiparticle with the same mass and opposite electric charge; for the electron it is the positron, for the proton the antiproton. Matter made of antiparticles is called antimatter [@dirac-1931 §1].

## Where the idea came from {#origin}

In 1928 Paul Dirac wrote an equation for the electron that combined quantum mechanics with special relativity and also had negative-energy solutions [@dirac-1928 §2]. In 1931 he interpreted these solutions as an unknown particle with the electron's mass and positive charge — an "anti-electron" [@dirac-1931 §1].

## How it was confirmed {#confirmed}

In 1933 Carl Anderson photographed cosmic-ray tracks in a cloud chamber that curved in a magnetic field like positively charged particles with the electron's mass [@anderson-1933 abstract]. It was a prediction made before the measurement, and it came true.

Today antimatter is produced and measured in the lab. In 2010 the ALPHA collaboration at CERN held antihydrogen atoms in a magnetic trap for the first time [@alpha-2010 abstract], and in 2023 it measured that antihydrogen falls down in a gravitational field like ordinary matter, within the precision of the experiment [@alpha-2023 abstract].

## What antimatter is not {#not}

Antimatter is not the same as [[term.dark-matter|dark matter]] or [[term.dark-energy|dark energy]]. We detect and produce antimatter directly; dark matter and dark energy are inferred from their gravitational effects but have not been caught directly [@lz-2023 abstract].
