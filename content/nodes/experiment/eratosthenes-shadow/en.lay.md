## What you will need {#needs .nocite}

- A vertical stick about 1 m long; check it with a plumb line or spirit level.
- A tape measure for the shadow length.
- A friend in another city at least 300 km north or south of you (the closer to the same meridian, the better).
- The time of local solar noon for both places (from an almanac or an app).

## Steps {#steps .nocite}

- Both of you set the stick upright on a level surface.
- At local solar noon, measure the length of the shadow.
- Each computes the Sun's angle from the vertical: angle = arctan(shadow length / stick height).
- The difference between the two angles is the fraction of a circle separating the cities on the globe.
- Earth's circumference = north–south distance between the cities × 360° / angle difference.

## What you will see {#you_will_see .nocite}

The shadow will be longer in the city closer to the pole. With an angle difference of about 3° and a distance of about 330 km, the circumference comes out around 40,000 km. An error of a few percent is normal — mostly from imperfect verticality and the estimate of noon.

## What it proves {#proves}

According to Cleomedes' later account, Eratosthenes started from the fact that at Syene at noon on the summer solstice vertical objects cast no shadow, while at Alexandria the shadow corresponded to one fiftieth of a circle. With 5,000 stadia between the cities, that gave a circumference of 250,000 stadia [@bowen-todd-2004 I.7].

The measurement assumes that Earth is round and that the Sun is so far away that its rays arrive practically parallel; under that assumption it yields the circumference. The reasoning was also an argument for roundness: on a flat Earth, shadows at the same moment would have the same angle everywhere unless the Sun were close [@bowen-todd-2004 I.7].

## What it does not prove {#does_not_prove}

Two cities alone cannot distinguish a round Earth from a flat Earth with a nearby Sun — both give different shadows. A third place or another observation decides it, for example Earth's round shadow on the Moon during an eclipse [@bowen-todd-2004 I.5].

Nor can the result in kilometres be compared directly with Eratosthenes' figure: exactly how many metres his stadion had is not known [@bowen-todd-2004 I.7].
