## What was known at the time {#context}

In the first half of the 19th century Uranus did not move exactly along the orbit that Newtonian gravity predicted with the known planets. The deviations were small, but larger than the measurement errors [@baum-sheehan-1997 ch. 4]. Newton's theory had until then reliably predicted planetary motions and the returns of comets, so it made no sense to look for the error in the theory first [@baum-sheehan-1997 ch. 1].

## Who proposed what {#who}

- Urbain Le Verrier calculated where an unknown planet would have to be to explain Uranus's deviations. Johann Galle found it in Berlin on 23 September 1846, using his figures, close to the predicted position [@galle-1846 p. 153].
- John Couch Adams carried out a similar calculation independently in England; the priority dispute is a story of its own [@baum-sheehan-1997 ch. 4].
- In 1859 the same Le Verrier showed that Mercury's perihelion advances a little faster than the known planets allow [@leverrier-1859 conclusion], and proposed the same remedy: an unseen planet or a ring of bodies closer to the Sun. The planet came to be called Vulcan [@baum-sheehan-1997 ch. 7].

## Objections {#objections}

At first an unseen planet was a reasonable choice: the same method had just worked for Uranus [@baum-sheehan-1997 ch. 7]. But reported sightings of Vulcan, such as that of the physician Edmond Lescarbault in 1859, could not be repeated, and searches during total solar eclipses produced nothing reliable [@baum-sheehan-1997 ch. 8].

## What decided it {#decided}

In November 1915 Albert Einstein calculated from general relativity that Mercury's perihelion advances by an extra 43″ per century compared with Newton — exactly the missing amount, with no extra planet [@einstein-1915 p. 839]. A later analysis by Gerald Clemence confirmed that the observed excess agrees with the relativistic prediction within the errors [@clemence-1947 abstract].

## How long it took {#duration}

Neptune: several decades passed from the first noticed deviations of Uranus to the discovery, but only a few weeks from Le Verrier's calculation to the find [@galle-1846 p. 153]. Vulcan: more than half a century, from 1859 to 1915, spent searching for a planet that was not there [@baum-sheehan-1997 ch. 10].

## The lesson — and where it stops applying {#lesson}

The same problem — motion that does not match the visible mass — had opposite solutions twice in history: once unseen matter (Neptune), once a new theory of gravity (Mercury) [@baum-sheehan-1997 ch. 10]. That is why today's dispute over [[obs.flat-rotation-curves|galaxy rotation curves]] cannot be settled by appeal to history: proponents of [[claim.dark-matter-halos|dark matter]] stand in Neptune's role, proponents of [[theory.mond]] in Mercury's [@milgrom-1983 abstract; @bertone-hooper-2018 §2]. What decides is whose predictions come true, not which analogy sounds better.
