## Myth and philosophy {#myth}

- Thales of Miletus (6th century BCE), according to later reports, held that water is the origin of everything [@kirk-raven-schofield-1983 ch. 2].
- Empedocles (5th century BCE) built the world from four "roots" — earth, water, air and fire — joined by Love and separated by Strife [@kirk-raven-schofield-1983 ch. 10].
- Leucippus and Democritus taught that everything is made of indivisible atoms moving in the void [@kirk-raven-schofield-1983 ch. 15].

These answers were philosophical reasoning, not measurement; we do not judge them as science but as early attempts to find a simple basis behind the variety of the world [@kirk-raven-schofield-1983 introduction].

## Historical models {#historical}

In the early 20th century stars were assumed to have a composition similar to Earth's. In her 1925 thesis Cecilia Payne derived from stellar spectra that hydrogen and helium dominate; she herself softened the result at the time because it seemed implausible [@payne-1925 ch. 13].

In 1933 Fritz Zwicky concluded from the motions of galaxies in the Coma cluster that it must contain far more matter than shines [@zwicky-1933 §5]. It was the first hint that visible matter might not be all there is [@bertone-hooper-2018 §3].

## Current science and its disputes {#science}

In the standard model [[theory.lcdm]], the density of the universe is about 5% ordinary matter (atoms), about 26% [[term.dark-matter|dark matter]] and about 69% [[term.dark-energy|dark energy]] [@planck-2018-vi tab. 2]. Only the first component is observed directly; the other two are inferred from gravitational effects and from the expansion of the universe [@bertone-hooper-2018 §1].

No experiment has yet caught a dark matter particle [@lz-2023 abstract]. Alternatives such as [[theory.mond]] explain part of the observations without dark matter but struggle with galaxy clusters [@sanders-2003 abstract]. [[term.antimatter|Antimatter]] is not one of the unknown components — it is produced and measured in the lab [@alpha-2010 abstract].

## How to tell whom to trust {#checklist .nocite}

- Did the author state the prediction before it was measured?
- Does the claim contain numbers that can be checked?
- Has someone else confirmed it, by a different method?
- Where is it published — a peer-reviewed paper, a preprint, or a book?
- What would convince the author that they are wrong?
