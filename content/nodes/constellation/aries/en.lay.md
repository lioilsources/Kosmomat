## What it is {#what}

Aries is a small constellation on the ecliptic — the path along which the Sun appears to move among the stars during the year — between Pisces and Taurus. Its exact boundaries, like those of all 88 constellations, were set by the International Astronomical Union in 1930 following Eugène Delporte's proposal [@delporte-1930 introduction].

A constellation is not a group of objects that belong together: stars we see side by side can lie at very different distances [@van-leeuwen-2007 §1].

## How far away it is {#distance}

Aries's brightest star, Hamal (α Arietis), is about 66 light years from us according to parallax measurements by the Hipparcos satellite [@van-leeuwen-2007 §1]. The light you see from it tonight left it roughly 66 years ago.

## What missions found {#missions}

The Hipparcos satellite (ESA, 1989–1993) measured the positions and distances of the stars of Aries and of the whole sky; a new reduction of its data in 2007 refined the parallaxes of bright stars [@van-leeuwen-2007 abstract].

## Sign and constellation {#sign_vs_constellation}

The sign of Aries and the constellation of Aries are not the same thing. Already in the 2nd century Ptolemy counted ecliptic longitudes from the vernal point — where the Sun stands on the day of the spring equinox [@toomer-1984 introduction]. The Western (tropical) zodiac is defined this way: the signs are twelve equal 30° segments starting at the vernal point, not constellations [@toomer-1984 introduction].

The vernal point slowly shifts against the stars — a phenomenon called precession, which according to Ptolemy was discovered by Hipparchus [@toomer-1984 VII.2]. The shift amounts to roughly one sign in about 2,000 years, so today the vernal point lies in the constellation Pisces and the Sun enters the constellation Aries about a month after the sign begins [@toomer-1984 VII.2; @delporte-1930 charts]. Astrologers are aware of this: Indian (Vedic) astrology uses a sidereal zodiac tied to the stars, Western astrology the tropical one [@pingree-1997 ch. 4].

## Open question {#open_question}

Exactly when the "Age of Aquarius" begins — when the vernal point crosses into the constellation Aquarius — depends on whose constellation boundaries we use; the IAU boundaries of 1930 give a different date than older charts. It is a question of definition, not of measurement [@delporte-1930 introduction].
